Product case study · Public health · Decision support

Helping public-health teams compare dengue interventions across connected cities.

An epidemiological simulation prototype that turns population movement, mosquito transmission, and observed case data into a scenario-planning concept.

Academic prototype · Not for operational use

The opportunity

Dengue response teams must make decisions while an outbreak is changing and people are moving between cities. A city with fewer current cases may still become a priority if it is strongly connected to an active outbreak.

How might we help a response team decide where and when to intervene when the available data is incomplete?

Product framing

Target users

Regional public-health planners, municipal vector-control teams, and hospital planning teams.

User need

Compare plausible outbreak trajectories and understand the consequences of acting earlier or later.

Product promise

Make scenario trade-offs visible before limited intervention resources are committed.

What I built

A transmission model

  • Human and mosquito compartments
  • Three interconnected fictional cities
  • Residents, workers, and city-to-city movement
  • Transmission and recovery parameters

A data and fitting workflow

  • SQLite patient snapshots for exploration
  • Aggregated city-level time-series data
  • Numerical integration with SciPy
  • Least-squares parameter fitting against observations

How the concept works

1

Aggregate the evidence

Convert patient snapshots into city-level infection and outcome time series.

2

Estimate the outbreak dynamics

Fit the model to observed data while representing mosquito transmission and population movement.

3

Compare scenarios

Run a baseline and intervention scenario, such as earlier treatment or reduced mosquito exposure.

4

Support a decision

Present peak timing, projected burden, and change versus baseline in a format a planning team can discuss.

Decision-oriented outputs

The next product layer would move beyond technical plots and make the model useful in a planning conversation.

Peak date When infections or hospitalizations are expected to peak
Burden Projected cases and severe outcomes by city
Delta Estimated reduction versus the no-intervention baseline

Product decisions and trade-offs

Optimize for comparison, not prediction theatre

The prototype should communicate that scenarios are useful for comparing choices, not for presenting false certainty about the future.

Expose assumptions

Every result should show the selected intervention, start date, parameters, data period, and model limitations.

Use a progressive disclosure model

Start with a recommendation summary and key metrics. Let technical users open the detailed curves, parameters, and methodology.

Keep humans in the loop

The product should support expert discussion and resource planning, never replace clinical or public-health judgment.

What I learned

A technically ambitious model does not automatically become a useful product. The value comes from connecting the model to a specific user, decision, workflow, and measurable outcome.

This project also made the importance of data definitions visible: “hospitalized,” “severe,” and “dead” are not interchangeable metrics. Clear terminology is a product-quality and model-quality requirement.
